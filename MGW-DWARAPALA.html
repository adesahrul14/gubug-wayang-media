DWARAPALA

Dwarapala pada mulanya merupakan Yaksa, sebangsa makhluk halus penguasa tanah. Ia kemudian dipuja sebagai pelindung kesuburan tanah guna mendatangkan sumber kehidupan. Saat agama Hindu dan Buddha berkembang di India, makhluk tersebut disejajarkan dengan kelompok setengah dewa, setingkat di bawah dewata. Ia ditugaskan mendampingi para dewata. Sebab itulah pada masa selanjutnya penggambarannya diletakkan di depan bangunan suci sebagai penjaga. Sesuai tugasnya, yaksa tersebut diberi nama Dwarapala, Sang Penjaga Pintu atau Penjaga Arah. Arca Dwarapala digambarkan duduk dengan kaki kiri ditekuk ke belakang dan kaki kanan ditekuk ke depan. Tangan kanannya memegang gada yang disandarkan pada paha kanan, sedang tangan kanannya menunjukkan mudra mengusir, ditandai dengan jari telunjuk dan jari tengah diacungkan serta sisanya ditekuk. Arca kedua digambarkan serupa dengan arca pertama, hanya saja terdapat perbedaan pada posisi kaki yang berlawanan dan tangannya tidak melakukan mudra mengusir, melainkan diletakkan di atas lutut. Arca digambarkan beratribut raya sesuai ketentuannya sebagai Dwarapala. Ia mengenakan tali selempang, kelat bahu, gelang tangan dan gelang kaki dari lilitan ular yang dalam bahasa ikonografi disebut sebagai naga. Terdapat pula kalung yang berupa untaian tengkorak dan manik-manik yang dirangkai indah mengelilingi leher hingga dada. Ikat dadanya digambarkan berupa rangkaian manik-manik dalam bidang berbentuk segitiga. Selanjutnya arca hanya mengenakan pakaian berupa kain sebatas perut hingga lutut.

Originally, Dwarapala was a Yaksha, a supernatural being believed to possess dominion over the earth. It was subsequently worshipped as a guardian of soil fertility, believed to bring forth sources of life. As Hinduism and Buddhism developed in India, this being came to be associated with a class of semi-divine beings ranked below the gods. It was assigned the role of accompanying and serving the deities. Consequently, in later periods, representations of this figure were placed in front of sacred buildings as guardians. In accordance with its function, the Yaksha was given the name Dwarapala, meaning the Gatekeeper or Guardian of the Directions. The Dwarapala statue is depicted in a seated position, with its left leg bent backward and its right leg bent forward. Its right hand holds a club (gada), which rests against the right thigh, while its left hand displays a gesture of warding off evil, characterized by the raised index and middle fingers and the remaining fingers bent inward. The second statue is depicted similarly to the first, with the only differences being the reversed leg positions and the absence of the warding-off gesture. Instead, its hand rests on the knee. The statues are depicted wearing elaborate attributes in accordance with their role as Dwarapala guardians. They are adorned with a shoulder sash, armlets, bracelets, and anklets fashioned in the form of coiled serpents, which are referred to as naga in iconographic terminology. They also wear necklaces consisting of strings of skulls and intricately arranged beads that encircle the neck and extend down to the chest. The chest ornament is depicted as an arrangement of beads forming a triangular pattern. Furthermore, the statues wear only a garment covering the body from the waist to the knees.<\p>
